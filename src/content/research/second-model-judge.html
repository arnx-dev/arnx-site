Research / Second opinion

Can a second LLM judge code review findings?

In our sample it was good at saying no, and poor at saying yes.

Partly. arnx asks a second model to read each failing test, the failure and the diff, and to confirm or dispute the finding. In one graded batch, a dispute was right 22 of 23 times. A confirmation held only 12 of 36 times. The two verdicts carry very different weight, and a review should be read that way.

One batch, one judge model, 59 judged findings. This is not a measure of second-model judging in general or of arnx as a product.

Data

The sample is the batch described in AI code review findings on unfamiliar repositories: 65 findings from 18 reviews of 12 open-source repositories, made with a pre-release build of arnx 0.1.8 and graded by us. The second opinion ran on GLM-5.3-Flash through River, with gpt-6.1-sol as the fallback when it timed out. Six findings failed on the code before the change too, so arnx did not send them to the second opinion. That leaves 59 judged findings.

The second opinion's verdict against our grade
Verdict Findings Real defect Not a defect Verdict was right
Confirmed 36 12 24 12 of 36
Disputed 23 1 22 22 of 23

The 12 confirmed findings that held were 3 real bugs and 9 real but minor defects. The 24 that did not hold were 13 tests of intended behavior, 9 unreachable scenarios and 2 wrong tests. The one dispute that was wrong hid a real, minor defect. Every finding graded a real bug had been confirmed.

Confirming and disputing are different jobs

To dispute a finding, the judge needs one solid reason: the input is invalid, the fixture is broken, or the assertion checks the wrong thing. Those reasons are often visible in the test and the diff, which is what the judge has in front of it.

To confirm a finding, the judge has to know that the test's expectation is what the project intends, and that real callers can reach the scenario. Neither is in the diff. Of the 24 wrong confirmations, 22 failed on exactly those two points: intent (13) and reachability (9). A second model that reads the same code as the first tends to share its guess about intent.

So agreement between two models is weak evidence, and disagreement is strong evidence. In this sample the judge worked as a filter for bad tests, not as a stamp of correctness.

What this means when you read a review

  • A disputed finding is probably not a bug. Read the reason. Skim it, and move on unless it names something you know to be false.
  • A confirmed finding is worth a look, not a verdict. Ask two questions the judge could not answer: is this what the code is meant to do, and can anything real reach it?
  • Your mark decides. arnx marks an issue backed by a confirmed test as real in advance. Change the mark when you disagree. Only tests of issues you leave marked real are kept.

This is also why arnx 0.1.11 does not fail a run on confirmed findings. arnx review exits 0 when confirmed findings exist: they are worth a look and do not fail the run. It exits 1 only for a finding the second opinion could not check, so a review never passes because the check did not run.

Defects we found in the judging step

Grading this batch exposed faults in how that build handled verdicts and labels. They bear on the numbers above.

  • A verdict copied from another case. When one test ran with several inputs and one case was disputed, the build applied that dispute to the other cases of the same test. In two findings it replaced a verdict where the judge had confirmed. In one of them the replaced confirmation was the right call: that is the one wrong dispute in the table.
  • Regression when the feature did not exist before. The build called a finding a regression whenever the test passed on the code before the change. Some of those tests passed there only because the feature was absent or a helper thread had died. The label then told the judge, and the reader, that the change broke something it had in fact added.
  • One judge named, another answered. A review's header named one second-opinion model. Some verdicts came from the fallback after a timeout. The per-finding lines named the right model; the header did not.

Later releases changed the before-and-after check: a pass on the old code now counts as a regression only when nothing shows the pass was empty. We have not regraded this batch on a later release, so we cannot say how the counts would move.

Replaying a judge

The arnx repository ships scripts/replay-judge.ts. Its header says it replays the second opinion on findings already graded, so a change to the judge is measured before it ships. It rebuilds each judge input from what a review saved, and the grades score the replies without ever being handed to a prompt.

Limitations

  • 59 judged findings, 18 reviews, 12 repositories, one judge model with one fallback. Another judge model could behave differently.
  • The grades are ours, made by AI graders we directed. No maintainer graded anything.
  • The judge saw labels that were sometimes wrong, as described above. Some wrong confirmations may be the label's fault more than the model's.
  • We did not test the judge alone on a fixed set with other models, and we did not measure how its verdict varies between runs.
  • TypeScript and Python only.

Conflict of interest

We build arnx. We ran the reviews and did the grading, and nobody outside checked it.

Artifacts

The second-opinion records, the grade sheet and the review files are kept by us and are not published yet. To reproduce the table a reader needs those files, the commit list and the build. The replay script above is public; the graded inputs it needs are not.
